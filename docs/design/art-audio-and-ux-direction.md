# Art Audio and UX Direction

[Project home](README.md) · Creative direction and proposed treatments. D-24 in the [decision register](decisions-and-open-questions.md) owns the confirmed console-era rendering direction. The initial procedural studies remain in [Style Board v0.1](style-board.md).

## Visual premise

A home maintained by people with different tastes, means, and repair habits. Equipment has understandable functions and visible modifications. Shared spaces should feel lived in before they become unsettling.

Explore worn timber, painted metal, salvaged furniture, handwritten revisions, cables that go somewhere, and objects repaired more than once. The confirmed setting is an alternate future after societal collapse. Treat these as reference directions rather than a fixed palette. Mix surviving future equipment with locally repaired objects coherently; avoid making the house generically mid-century by default. Avoid assigning a uniform occult aesthetic to every resident.

## Telecommunications refuge and household attitude

The home is a converted telecommunications office. Proposed details include service counters, cable maps, equipment cabinets, corporate notices, and meeting-room furniture repurposed for communal living. Preserve signs of different residents' tastes and the building's former use.

DedSec from Watch Dogs 2 is the confirmed reference for aesthetic and attitude. Proposed expressions include irreverent homemade graphics, station identifiers, stickers, conspicuous repairs, and humor about obsolete authority. Residents need not share one style or worldview. This cultural influence does not supersede the low-poly console-era treatment selected in D-24.

Case aftermath can alter small visible or audible details: a photograph moves, an evening check-in gains a name, a familiar chair remains empty, or a resident resumes communal meals. Such traces belong to authored stories; they do not authorize additional final assets or baseline mechanics.

## Space and lighting

Use readable routes between radio room, kitchen, and archive/workshop. Establish each room's ordinary appearance before introducing changes. Warm household light and cooler equipment illumination are a possible contrast, not a mandatory color code for safety and danger.

Keep documents and controls legible in low-light scenes. Provide a close-inspection view and scalable text. Atmospheric darkness should not make essential clues inaccessible.

## Radio and environmental sound

Build distinct layers: room tone, equipment hum, channel noise, transmitted speech, and nearby household activity. Establish recurring sounds such as the kitchen bell so recognition can support investigation. Filter radio speech without destroying intelligibility. Important dialogue must not be masked by a simultaneous scare or loud household sound.

Spatial sound can make the home tangible: footsteps on a familiar stair, a cupboard closing, somebody moving a chair to join you. Use silence and absence deliberately after ordinary routines have become familiar.

Separate voice, music, ambience, and radio-noise levels. Supply subtitles and sound captions for consequential cues. A clean playback mode may reduce filtering while retaining the evidence's meaning.

Ordinary network programming includes music, news, and talk shows. Give broadcasts recognizable habits before introducing discrepancies. Any future security feed, call, chat, or monitor retains its original perspective and coverage. Provide captions, transcripts, and equivalent descriptions for essential evidence; displaced media are not an unrestricted visual memory.

## Suspense and memories

Favor subtle changes, uncertain signal origins, and environmental discrepancies over startling volume peaks or repeated jump scares. Preserve ordinary warmth and readable spaces.

For investigative memories, establish a consistent entry and return cue. Make the source or viewpoint legible without assuming that all remembered details are true. An earlier state of an existing room is a proposed POC experiment, reducing new asset work for the solo developer.

## Interface

Keep frequent radio operations available at the desk. Present recordings, logs, and comparisons in a legible notebook/archive interface. Essential information should remain available outside a purely physical simulation of paper handling.

Show focus, current channel, recording state, route destination, and send status clearly. Tuning and routing need alternatives to precise dragging. A comparison view preserves the source and timestamp of each excerpt.

## Accessibility baseline for the prototype

- Text scaling, speaker labels, complete dialogue subtitles, and captions for meaningful sounds.
- Equivalent visual/text evidence for audio recognition clues; captions describe observable sounds without solving their significance.
- No required rapid input, pitch discrimination, color-only distinctions, or timed dialogue.
- Keyboard navigation for every essential action; remappable input planned before a wider playtest.
- Adjustable mouse sensitivity and options to disable head bob and camera shake.
- Pause, replay, and save/load support during investigation.

Controller support, screen-reader integration, platform-specific accessibility, and localization require dedicated feasibility work. They are not claimed as implemented or fully scoped.

## Prototype asset budget

Use blockout geometry for three rooms, two simple resident representations, one radio panel, one routing switch, a tape player/archive interface, and a small collection of case documents. Temporary recorded or text-delivered dialogue is sufficient. Maintain captions from the first content pass.

No final character models, cinematic sequences, licensed music, or full voice casting are required to answer the first design questions. Record rights and provenance for any external asset before incorporating it into a distributable build.

## Questions for an eventual style board

How much material realism serves legibility? How can the equipment communicate an alternate future and a history of repair after collapse? Which personal objects communicate differences between residents? How can one altered routine create unease without a visual horror effect? Resolve these through a small number of scene studies after the setting direction is chosen.
